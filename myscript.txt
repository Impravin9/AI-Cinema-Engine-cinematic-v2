In World War II, an army made of rubber and wood would fool Hitler at the moment it mattered most. Leningrad would endure 872 days of siege, while Denmark fell in just 6 hours. But the strangest part is how it began 20 years earlier, Hitler's Germany had an army of 100,000 men and not a single tank. The people who could have stopped him let him through again and again. So what happened in those 20 years It all started in 1919 at Versailles, with a peace Germany was never allowed to negotiate. Germany lost 13% of its territory and all of its colonies. It was stripped of its air force and its submarines. Article 231 declared it guilty for the entire war. On top of that came a debt of 132 billion gold marks. How does a country that just lost four years of war pay that The German government found a very practical answer print money. By 1923, paper money was worth so little that people burned it in their stoves, because it was cheaper than buying firewood. Anyone with savings watched them turn into fuel. With the treaty on their shoulders and their savings gone, Germans looked for someone to blame. That same year, in Munich, an army corporal named Adolf Hitler tried to seize power. The coup failed within hours, and he ended up in prison. There he dictated Mein Kampf, blaming Jews and communists for the defeat and promising to restore Germany's greatness. But a coup was going nowhere, so he changed method he walked out of prison and ran for election. In 1928, the Nazi Party won about 810,000 votes, under 3%, and 12 seats in parliament. Then, in 1929, the New York stock market collapsed. Banks failed one after another, and millions of Germans lost their jobs. A country that hadn't finished paying for the last crisis was hit by another, and desperate people don't vote for whoever asks for patience, but for whoever promises a culprit. In 1930, the Nazis jumped from 810,000 votes to about 6.4 million, almost eight times more in two years. Now the conservatives who despised him began to need his votes. And they asked themselves what if we bring him inside the government and he calms down In January 1933, Hitler was appointed chancellor, and those who put him there were sure they could control him. In under two months, the Enabling Act gave him absolute power by 444 votes to 94. German democracy was dead, and parliament itself had buried it with a vote. Now the question was whether anyone outside Germany would do anything. The first test came in a region right next to France, and Hitler had already decided how much to risk there. On March 7, 1936, about 20,000 German soldiers crossed the bridges over the Rhine into the Rhineland, where the treaty forbade them to be. France had a far stronger army and still had time to stop them. It was the cheapest moment there would ever be a few French regiments moving to the border would have ended Hitler's gamble in an afternoon. France protested. Britain protested. Neither moved a single soldier. Years later, Hitler admitted that if France had reacted, he would have had to retreat. He had been bluffing, and nobody asked him to show his cards. He learned the cheapest lesson of the decade, and others would pay the bill. And he wasn't the only one testing the world. Japan had invaded Manchuria in 1931 and launched total war on China in 1937, and Italy had invaded Ethiopia in 1935. The League of Nations, created so wars wouldn't happen again, protested all three times and did nothing three times. Three aggressors, three weak responses. Who would stand up to Hitler if nobody had stood up to the others In March 1938, Germany annexed Austria, and France and Britain again did nothing. There wasn't even surprise anymore, just routine two years, two borders changed by force, and no answer. Next came Czechoslovakia, specifically the Sudetenland, a border region with a large German-speaking population that Hitler claimed for Germany. In September 1938, British prime minister Chamberlain and French premier Daladier met Hitler in Munich to decide the fate of a country that wasn't even invited. After the bloodshed of World War I, London and Paris preferred to hand over the Sudetenland rather than risk another war. Czechoslovakia was left out of the negotiations and learned the result only after the deal was done. In exchange, Hitler promised this would be his last territorial demand. Chamberlain returned to London proclaiming peace for our time. Six months later, Hitler occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia. The Sudetenland had been the pledge, and he took the rest of the house too. Churchill called Munich a total defeat. The last demand had lasted six months. What Munich bought wasn't peace but time, and the next installment already had a name Poland. But for that, Hitler needed something more than the passivity of France and Britain he needed Stalin not to move. How do you buy the calm of a declared enemy with a piece of paper In August 1939, Germany and the Soviet Union signed a non-aggression pact. The key wasn't in the public text but in a secret clause to divide Poland and the Baltic states between them. Nobody expected it, because they were declared enemies. With his back covered in the east, Hitler could attack without fearing a two-front war. At dawn on September 1, 1939, German tanks crossed into Poland, followed by over a million and a half soldiers. On September 3, France and Britain declared war on Germany, as they had promised. But declaring war was one thing and fighting it was another no offensive came to relieve the Poles. On September 17, the Soviet Union invaded from the east, and the pact between Hitler and Stalin moved from paper to reality. Trapped between two armies, Poland fell in little more than a month. What followed was the strangest part of the war almost nothing. For months the Western Front stayed quiet, and journalists called it the Phony War. French soldiers spent the winter behind the Maginot Line, a chain of bunkers, guns and tunnels along the German border. It's often called a stupid wall, but it wasn't it was so good that no sane commander wanted to smash into it. French generals were so sure of it that they had already decided where to expect the attack. While France waited, Hitler didn't. In April 1940, Denmark fell in 6 hours, less than an office workday, and Germany invaded Norway. On May 10, he attacked France, Belgium and the Netherlands all at once. The French sent their best troops into Belgium to meet the blow. But the blow came through the Ardennes, a forest of narrow roads their generals thought impassable for tanks. The Panzers crossed it in 10 days and reached the English Channel, splitting the Allied armies in two. Trapped in the north, the British and part of the French army had the sea at their backs and the Germans ahead. They had one port left Dunkirk. For nine days, warships, fishing boats and private yachts crossed back and forth, pulling 338,000 soldiers off the beach. It was an escape, not a victory, and Churchill reminded everyone that wars are not won by evacuations. France fell in six weeks, and Hitler made it sign the surrender in the same railway car where Germany had signed its own in 1918, a choice made on purpose. In summer 1940, only one country stood in his way, about 30 kilometers of sea away Britain. As long as the Royal Air Force stayed in the air, German landing barges would be floating targets, so Hitler ordered the Luftwaffe to destroy it first. In London, some politicians thought negotiating was sensible, but Churchill wouldn't hear of it We shall fight on the beaches. We shall never surrender. What stopped the bombers was what Britain had built years earlier radar towers along the south coast that saw German planes while still over the sea. Pilots took off just in time, in a fast, agile fighter, the Spitfire. The Luftwaffe lost around 1,800 aircraft and failed to destroy the RAF. Hitler shelved the invasion. It was the first time anyone had told him no. But he didn't stay staring at the sea. If he couldn't win by air, he would win on land, and he looked east, toward the country he had signed a pact with. On June 22, 1941, Germany broke the pact and launched the largest land invasion in history over 3 million soldiers in three army groups, toward Leningrad, Moscow and Ukraine. Hitler expected quick victories to collapse the Soviet Union, but the Germans kept encircling huge forces only to find new units taking their place. In the north, unable to enter Leningrad, they tried to starve it a siege of 872 days, with between 800,000 and 1.5 million dead, mostly from hunger. The few supplies came over frozen Lake Ladoga, which the people called the Road of Life. But Hitler's real enemy was the calendar winter caught German soldiers in summer uniforms, and outside Moscow they suffered around 750,000 casualties. While they froze, a Japanese fleet was sailing silently toward Hawaii. On a sunny Sunday morning, December 7, 1941, Pearl Harbor was attacked, 19 ships were sunk or damaged, and 2,403 Americans died. Roosevelt told Congress it was a date which will live in infamy, the U.S. declared war on Japan, and Germany and Italy declared war on the U.S. Two separate wars had become one world war, and in six months Japan conquered the Philippines, the Dutch East Indies and Burma, and took 80,000 prisoners in Singapore. But American codebreakers had a hidden card a fake message that Midway was running out of water confirmed Japan's target, and in June 1942, American dive bombers sank four Japanese carriers in a single day. Then came Stalingrad, where Hitler forbade his Sixth Army to break out of the Soviet encirclement, Göring's promised 300 tons of daily air supply turned into about 94, and on February 2, 1943, the survivors surrendered; of roughly 91,000 prisoners, only about 5,000 came home. From there the Allies landed in Sicily, fooled Hitler with a rubber-tank army and stormed Normandy on June 6, 1944, until Berlin fell and Hitler took his own life on April 30, 1945. Hiroshima and Nagasaki ended the war with 70 to 85 million dead, and Einstein, whose signature on a 1939 letter started it all, called it the greatest mistake of his life.
